A Basketball Example of Non-Identically Distributed Observations

Modeling fatigue with changing shot probabilities

Author

Math 167R

1 The modeling question

Suppose a basketball player takes 20 shots during a game. We want to model fatigue: the player begins the game shooting very well, but becomes less likely to make a shot as the game continues.

For example, suppose the probability of making the first shot is 0.90 and the probability of making the twentieth shot is 0.50.

The question is:

What happens to the distribution of the total number of made shots when the probability of success changes from shot to shot?

2 An important notation clarification

In a binomial distribution,

\[ Y \sim \operatorname{Binomial}(n,p), \]

the parameter \(n\) means the total number of trials. In this example, the player takes exactly 20 shots, so the total number of trials is fixed:

\[ n=20. \]

The shot number increases from 1 to 20. We will call that shot number \(t\), and the probability of making shot \(t\) will be \(p_t\).

Thus, the model has:

  • a fixed number of shots, \(n=20\);
  • an increasing shot number, \(t=1,2,\ldots,20\); and
  • a decreasing probability, \(p_t\).

3 Independent but non-identically distributed shots

Let

\[ X_t = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if shot }t\text{ is made},\\ 0 & \text{if shot }t\text{ is missed}. \end{cases} \]

Each shot is a Bernoulli random variable:

\[ X_t \sim \operatorname{Bernoulli}(p_t). \]

The shots are assumed to be independent, meaning the result of one shot does not directly affect the result of another shot. However, they are not identically distributed, because their probabilities are different:

\[ p_1 \ne p_2 \ne \cdots \ne p_{20}. \]

This is called an independent but non-identically distributed process, often abbreviated as independent non-identically distributed, or i.n.i.d.

4 Creating a linearly decreasing probability

We can use a linear sequence to decrease the probability from 0.90 to 0.50:

n_shots <- 20

shot_number <- 1:n_shots

p <- seq(
  from = 0.90,
  to = 0.50,
  length.out = n_shots
)

data.frame(
  shot = shot_number,
  probability = round(p, 3)
)

The first probability is 0.90, the last probability is 0.50, and the probabilities between them decrease evenly.

The average probability is

\[ \frac{0.90+0.50}{2}=0.70. \]

That does not mean every shot has probability 0.70. It means the average of the 20 different probabilities is 0.70.

5 Simulating one game

Since each shot is a Bernoulli trial, we can use rbinom() with size = 1. The argument prob = p supplies a different probability for each shot.

set.seed(123)

made <- rbinom(
  n = n_shots,
  size = 1,
  prob = p
)

one_game <- data.frame(
  shot = shot_number,
  probability = round(p, 3),
  made = made
)

The variable made contains 1 for a made shot and 0 for a missed shot. The total number of made shots is:

[1] 14

The player’s shooting percentage for this game is:

mean(made)
[1] 0.7

Because this is a random simulation, the result will not usually equal the average probability exactly.

6 Plotting the model and one simulated game

The red line shows the probability of making each shot. The blue points show what actually happened in one simulated game.

plot(shot_number, p,
     type = "b",
     pch = 19,
     ylim = c(0, 1),
     col = "firebrick",
     xlab = "Shot number",
     ylab = "Probability / outcome",
     main = "Shooting Probability Decreases with Fatigue")

points(shot_number, made,
       pch = 19,
       col = "steelblue",
       cex = 1.2)

legend("topright",
       legend = c("Probability of a make", "Actual outcome"),
       col = c("firebrick", "steelblue"),
       lty = c(1, NA),
       pch = 19,
       bty = "n")

The actual outcomes jump between 0 and 1 because each shot either is made or missed. The probabilities change gradually because fatigue is modeled as a gradual process.

7 Why this is relevant to the Central Limit Theorem

The usual introductory version of the Central Limit Theorem assumes that the observations are independent and identically distributed. This basketball example keeps independence but removes identical distribution:

Condition Does the fatigue model satisfy it?
Independent shots Yes, by assumption
Identically distributed shots No, because \(p_t\) changes
Finite means and variances Yes
Large number of trials Not necessarily, when one game has only 20 shots

For one game, 20 shots may not be enough to produce a visibly normal distribution of total makes. However, if we increase the number of shots or study sums of many independent non-identically distributed trials, normal approximations may still become reasonable under additional conditions.

This gives students a useful simulation question:

Does a normal approximation work similarly when the Bernoulli probabilities are constant versus when they change over time?

8 Summary

In this model, the player takes 20 shots, but the probability of making a shot changes with the shot number:

\[ X_t \sim \operatorname{Bernoulli}(p_t), \qquad p_1=0.90,\quad p_{20}=0.50. \]

The shots are independent but not identically distributed. The total number of makes is therefore Poisson-binomial rather than ordinary binomial. The expected total is the sum of the individual probabilities, and the variance is the sum of the individual Bernoulli variances.

This is a realistic and accessible example for studying how changing probabilities affect sampling distributions and the assumptions behind the Central Limit Theorem.
